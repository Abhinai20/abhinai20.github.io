What a Unix timestamp is

A Unix timestamp counts the seconds elapsed since 00:00:00 UTC on 1 January 1970, known as the epoch. It is a single integer with no timezone attached, which is what makes it convenient to store and compare — and what makes it easy to misread.

A ten-digit number is almost certainly seconds. A thirteen-digit number is milliseconds. Recognising which you are holding is usually just a matter of counting the digits.

Useful reference points

  • 0 — 1 January 1970, the epoch itself.
  • 1000000000 — 9 September 2001.
  • 1600000000 — 13 September 2020.
  • 2000000000 — 18 May 2033.
  • 2147483647 — 19 January 2038, the signed 32-bit limit.

Gotchas worth knowing

Seconds versus milliseconds

This is the most common bug in this area. JavaScript's Date.now() returns milliseconds; most Unix tooling, database functions and JWT claims use seconds. Mixing them does not usually throw an error — it silently produces a date in 1970 or in the year 54,000, which then propagates into comparisons and expiry checks. If a date looks absurd by a factor of a thousand, this is why.

The year 2038 problem

A signed 32-bit integer runs out at 03:14:07 UTC on 19 January 2038. Systems still storing time that way will wrap to a negative number and read as 1901. Modern 64-bit platforms are fine, but embedded devices, old database schemas and file formats with fixed-width fields are not automatically safe — and anything calculating far-future dates, such as a long-lived certificate or a retention policy, can hit the ceiling well before 2038 arrives.

A timestamp has no timezone, but your display does

The integer itself is unambiguous and always UTC. Every conversion to a human-readable string applies a timezone somewhere, and the default is usually the machine's local setting. The same timestamp shown on a developer laptop and a production server in another region produces two different strings, neither of which is wrong. Store and transmit UTC; convert only at the point of display.

Leap seconds are not represented

Unix time pretends every day is exactly 86,400 seconds, so inserted leap seconds are not counted. A timestamp is therefore not a true count of elapsed physical seconds since 1970. This almost never matters, but it does mean two timestamps subtracted give you a nominal interval rather than a precise duration — and it is why high-precision systems smear leap seconds rather than inserting them.

Do not use it to measure elapsed time

Wall-clock time can jump backwards when NTP corrects drift or a timezone database updates, which can make a measured duration negative. For timing how long something took, use a monotonic clock instead — time.monotonic() in Python, performance.now() in browsers, CLOCK_MONOTONIC on Linux.
